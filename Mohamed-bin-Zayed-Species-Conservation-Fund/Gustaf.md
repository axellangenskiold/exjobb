## Section 4: Overview of your individual project

**4.1 Organisation details** (max 500 words)

> This is an individual research project carried out by two MSc students in Computer Science at Lund University, Sweden, in partnership with Elephants for Africa, a conservation research organisation that has studied male African savanna elephants in the Makgadikgadi, Botswana, since 2012.
>
> **Mission:** to give conservation authorities and communities in the Makgadikgadi reliable, seasonal maps of where bull elephants are likely to move, and to find out whether the bulls' need for minerals such as sodium draws them along particular routes, including out of protected areas and into farmland.
>
> **Objectives:** (1) model how bull elephants choose where to move, using existing hourly GPS data from around ten bulls collared by Elephants for Africa since 2022; (2) test whether mineral-rich soils and salt pans shape those choices beyond water and vegetation; (3) simulate the bulls' movements to identify the corridors they use; (4) check the mineral maps against soil samples collected along the predicted corridors; and (5) hand the maps, code and training over to Elephants for Africa and the Department of Wildlife and National Parks.
>
> **Founded:** the project starts in February 2027 and forms our master's thesis, supervised by Jonas Skeppstedt (Computer Science) and Johan Lindström (Mathematical Statistics) at Lund University, with Dr Kate Evans of Elephants for Africa as research partner. We will work from Elephants for Africa's research camp near Khumaga on the Boteti River from 15 February to 1 June 2027.
>
> **Geographic area:** Makgadikgadi Pans National Park, the Boteti River, and the community and farming areas around Khumaga, Central District, Botswana.
>
> **Target species and habitat:** the African savanna elephant (*Loxodonta africana*), listed as Endangered by the IUCN, and specifically the adult bulls that return to the Boteti River and the salt pans each year.
>
> **Why it matters:** Botswana holds the world's largest elephant population, and elephants increasingly move through farmland. Between 2018 and 2022, elephants caused 41% of all wildlife damage to crops, boreholes, water tanks and fences in Botswana. Elephants for Africa's collared bulls spend about three times as much time outside protected areas as inside them. Knowing when and where bulls are likely to move lets farmers and wildlife officers prepare, for example by protecting water points or placing deterrents along predicted routes.
>
> Elephants need sodium, which their plant diet lacks, so they seek out salty soils. Whether this drives the bulls' movements has not yet been tested. If it does, managing mineral sources, such as placing salt licks inside the park, becomes a practical tool to keep bulls away from farmland.
>
> **Approach:** step selection models compare each step a bull took with the steps it could have taken, measuring what bulls prefer. The models then simulate thousands of virtual bull tracks to reveal corridors, and are tested on how well they predict where real bulls go 1 to 12 months ahead. A small soil sampling campaign (up to 20 samples) along the predicted corridors checks the mineral maps on the ground. No animals are handled or disturbed.

**5.3 Location details** (max 200 words)

> The project focuses on Makgadikgadi Pans National Park and the land around it in Central District, Botswana. The park's western boundary follows the Boteti River, which began flowing again in 2009; since then, adult bull elephants have returned to the river in large numbers each dry season. East of the river lie the Ntwetwe and Sowa salt pans and the grasslands between them, and west of it are the villages, cattle posts and fields around Khumaga, where people and elephants come into conflict. The collared bulls range across the park and the community land around it, and some have travelled as far as Zimbabwe (Vader 2023); the GPS data from 2022 to 2025 cover this whole range. Fieldwork is based at Elephants for Africa's research camp near Khumaga. Soil samples will be collected along corridors predicted by the models, inside and next to the park.

**5.4 Conservation status and habitat importance** (max 200 words)

> The African savanna elephant was listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List in 2021 (Gobush et al. 2021), after the population fell by at least 60% over the last 50 years, mainly through poaching and habitat loss. Botswana holds the world's largest population, so how Botswana's elephants and people share the land matters for the species as a whole. In the Makgadikgadi, bulls depend on the Boteti River for water in the dry season, but the land west of the river is farmed, and elephants caused 41% of all wildlife damage to crops and water infrastructure in Botswana between 2018 and 2022 (ATC News 2023). Retaliation and loss of tolerance are therefore major threats. The salt pans make this landscape unusual: they are among the largest in the world and give strong contrasts in soil minerals, which elephants need because their plant diet is low in sodium (Holdø et al. 2002). Understanding whether bulls move between the river, pans and farmland to meet mineral needs shows which habitats and corridors must be protected, and where mineral sources could be managed to keep bulls inside the park.

**5.7 Project objectives** (max 200 words)

**5.8 Relation to previous work** (max 400 words)

> Elephants for Africa has studied male elephants in the Makgadikgadi since 2012, focusing on their social behaviour and competition with people for resources (Evans 2019). In 2022 it collared around ten adult bulls with hourly GPS collars. One aim of that project was to learn whether mineral needs drive the bulls' movements (AZA n.d.), and the collars showed that the bulls spend about three times as much time outside protected areas as inside (Elephants for Africa 2025). A first analysis of ten months of the data (Vader 2023) found that home range sizes vary widely between bulls, that bulls prefer shrubland and roads and avoid crops and bare ground, and that a bull in musth moved faster and further. It did not include minerals. Our project uses the full 2022 to 2025 data and answers the question the collaring was designed for.
>
> Minerals are a known but rarely modelled driver of elephant movement. Elephants eat soil where their food is low in sodium (Holdø et al. 2002), and sodium may limit large herbivores across Africa, with animals travelling far for salt risking more conflict with people (Abraham et al. 2025).
>
> Our methods follow established and recent movement ecology. Step selection functions compare each observed step with the steps an animal could have taken (Fortin et al. 2005); integrated versions also model step length and direction so that movement can be simulated (Avgar et al. 2016; Signer et al. 2017); mixed-effects versions allow each animal its own preferences (Muff et al. 2020). Osipova et al. (2019) used step selection to map elephant connectivity, and a deep learning version recently outperformed conventional models (Forrest et al. 2026). Mineral layers combine 30 m soil nutrient maps (Hengl et al. 2021) with literature, geological and mining-industry data.
>
